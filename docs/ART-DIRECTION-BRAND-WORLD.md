# Color & Scent — Art Direction / Brand-World Memo

## Executive recommendation

Position Color & Scent as **“the candlelit color atelier”**: premium and cozy, but not beige; modern and editorial, but not sterile. The distinctive territory is tactile, human, color-coded scent memory: colored wax/vessels, linen paper, imperfect shadows, hands, table corners, botanicals, pigment swatches, and small domestic rituals. The site should feel less like a Shopify template and more like a photographed desk from a perfumer-interior stylist: a warm room, a color card, a handwritten scent note, a candle burning nearby.

The premium candle market splits into three useful lanes: heritage perfumery minimalism (Diptyque/Trudon), clean accessible premium (Maison Louis Marie, Brooklyn Candle Studio, APOTHEKE), and expressive color/culture-led DTC (Boy Smells, Otherland, LOEWE Home Scents).[1][3][9] Diptyque foregrounds proprietary type and black/white luxury codes; Trudon lives at the high end with candles from roughly $50–$700; Maison Louis Marie lists $40 candles with numbered scents and elegant italic editorial typography.[1][3][9] APOTHEKE’s classic candles are $46–$48 and emphasize perfume-grade fragrance, vegan soy wax blend, cruelty-free, hand-poured candles; Boy Smells uses black vessels, colorful labels, scent-family filters, and a bolder typographic system; LOEWE proves that saturated vessel color can be luxury, not novelty.[2][5][6]

## The white-space

Most competitors use one of two defaults: **black/white clinical luxury** or **warm-neutral apothecary calm**. Color & Scent should not join either default. Own **colored atmosphere**: every scent has a color logic, but the color is expressed through materials and light rather than flat website gradients.

**Design idea:** each fragrance becomes a small interior palette:
- a wax/vessel color,
- a printed label color,
- a paper swatch,
- a room/material pairing,
- 3 scent notes written as a mini scene.

This connects directly to the stated opportunity around color-coded scent collections and a custom candle builder, but makes it feel premium rather than gimmicky.

## Moodboard references / what to borrow

1. **Diptyque:** borrow restraint, recognizability, and the idea of proprietary label architecture; do not borrow black-and-white Parisian pastiche.[1]
2. **Maison Louis Marie:** borrow airy editorial product cards, italic refinement, numbered scents, and note-forward naming; avoid the over-soft, almost generic wellness look.[3]
3. **Boy Smells:** borrow confident color-coded labels, scent-family navigation, and pop cultural naming; avoid their high-contrast graphic loudness for Color & Scent.[2]
4. **LOEWE Home Scents:** borrow luxury saturation, sculptural vessel presence, and the permission to make color the premium object; avoid looking like ceramic-fashion luxury unless the vessel quality can support it.[6]
5. **APOTHEKE / Brooklyn Candle Studio:** borrow accessible premium clarity and material/product-truth copy — soy, hand-poured, perfume-grade, living room fill — but avoid their common black/white/neutral shelf language.[4][5]
6. **Trudon:** borrow the sense that candles can belong to interiors, dinners, rituals, and decorative objects; do not borrow baroque heritage unless the brand earns it.[9]
7. **Otherland:** borrow “design meets fragrance” and artful candle-as-emotional-journey positioning; avoid overly graphic illustration if the goal is tactile and editorial.[10]

## Photography direction

**Core photographic rule:** every image needs evidence of touch, temperature, or material. No floating product renders. No generic clean gradient boxes. No stock lifestyle.

### Product photography
- Shoot on **warm plaster, raw linen, travertine, stained oak, cream paper, and colored vellum**.
- Use **single-window light plus candlelight**, not glossy studio lighting. Shadows should be soft but present.
- Product grid images can remain clean, but use a slightly warm off-white background (#F4EEE6 / #EFE7DC), visible grounding shadow, and consistent angle.
- Add one tactile prop per scent family: torn paper strip, ceramic tile, textile fold, dried botanical, fruit peel, tea spoon, smoke match, color pencil, folded card.
- Use macro crops: wax surface, wick texture, flame glow reflected in glass, paper label tooth, embossed box edge.

### Editorial/lifestyle photography
Create recurring brand-world sets:
1. **The Color Table:** candle, color swatches, scent ingredients, matches, small domestic object.
2. **The Evening Shelf:** candle burning on a shelf with books, ceramics, flowers, framed art — never a hotel-spa bathroom.
3. **The Builder Desk:** hands selecting vessel color, label color, scent notes, ribbon/paper.
4. **The Room Mood:** not full-room interior design; close fragments of home — curtain shadow, bedside wood, linen sofa arm, kitchen marble, reading nook.
5. **The Gift Moment:** box tissue, handwritten note, colored seal, open carton, candle partially wrapped.

### Camera language
- 50mm/85mm feel, shallow but not blurry everywhere.
- Mostly square and 4:5 crops; occasional wide editorial banners.
- Human presence through hands, cuffs, shadows, match striking, wrapping — not posed models smelling candles.
- Warm black levels; avoid gray digital blacks.

## Typography direction

The category already leans serif/display across competitors, so use a more ownable pairing:

- **Display serif:** a warm, bookish, slightly calligraphic serif for scent names and editorial headlines. Think Canela, Reckless, Editorial New, or Cormorant Garamond if budget-constrained.
- **Functional sans:** a quiet humanist/grotesk for UI and body. Think Suisse Int’l, Neue Haas Grotesk, Untitled Sans, or Inter as a fallback.
- **Micro mono or engraved small caps:** for formula-like details: burn time, wax weight, scent note pyramid, batch number, “poured on,” “color family.” Diptyque’s page exposes custom typography including Apercu Pro Mono and a proprietary Diptyque face, proving that small typographic systems create luxury recognition beyond just a logo.[1]

Avoid giant all-caps luxury headers. Use lowercase/Title Case conversational headings. Let scent names be expressive; let UX be very clear.

## Color and material palette

### Base palette
- **Warm Milk** #F6F0E7 — main background, not pure white.
- **Candlewick Black** #171412 — text, icons, label type.
- **Linen Taupe** #C9BCAE — cards, borders, tissue.
- **Soft Clay** #B8795D — warmth, CTA hover, editorial panels.
- **Smoke Olive** #74725F — secondary text/accent.
- **Wax Cream** #FFF5D8 — candlelight glow.

### Signature scent-color families
Use muted, interior-friendly colors, not web-bright colors:
- **Amber / gourmand:** ochre, burnt sugar, toasted cream.
- **Floral / soft:** dusty rose, mauve plaster, faded lilac.
- **Fresh / citrus:** citron peel, pale chartreuse, mineral mint.
- **Woody / smoky:** cedar brown, ash gray, moss green.
- **Clean / linen:** chalk blue, warm white, stone.
- **Custom blend:** split-label or two-tone wax ring showing selected colors.

### Materials
- frosted or semi-translucent glass,
- matte colored glass or ceramic for premium line,
- uncoated paper label with deboss/letterpress,
- cotton wick and natural wax surface visible,
- kraft outer shipper but premium inner box,
- colored tissue or belly band per fragrance family.

## Label and packaging implications

The label system should be the brand’s most ownable asset.

**Recommended front label architecture:**
- top: Color & Scent wordmark,
- middle: large scent name or custom blend name,
- left/right: small color chip + scent family,
- bottom: 3-note line and candle size,
- tiny detail: batch/date/poured location.

**Make labels color-coded but editorial:** not a big solid sticker with loud color. Use a cream paper label with a **paint-chip block, colored edge, or vertical color rule**. For custom candles, print a dual-color chip and “Blended for ___” line. This supports gifting and the builder without making packaging look personalized in a cheap way.

**Box direction:** warm off-white rigid or folding carton, black/soft-clay type, color strip on one side, interior tissue in scent color. The unboxing should photograph well: tissue, label, matchbook, note card, care card.

**Premium upgrade:** a reusable colored vessel line. LOEWE demonstrates that color itself can be the luxury vessel language; Color & Scent can do this accessibly with frosted/matte glass before moving into ceramic.[6]

## Site/UI art direction

The site should be editorial-commerce, not template-commerce.

- Hero: candlelit close crop + headline “Find the color your room is missing.” CTA: “Build your candle” / “Shop the collection.”
- Product grid: warm background, generous gutters, scent-family chips, quick note line.
- PDP: large material photography, scent as room/palette, “smells like” micro-scene, color swatch, notes, burn/care details.
- Builder: tactile steps: Choose a room mood → choose scent notes → choose color → name it → gift note. Visually show stacked swatches rather than form fields.
- Editorial modules: “Color stories,” “Room recipes,” “How to burn a candle properly,” “Gifting by mood.”

## Microcopy / brand voice

Voice: warm, sensory, specific, lightly poetic. Never mystical. Never wellness-bro. Never luxury cosplay.

### Copy principles
- Describe scent through **places, textures, and rituals**, not only notes.
- Use short phrases with one concrete image.
- Make color useful: “for late-afternoon rooms,” “for linen sheets,” “for a kitchen after rain.”
- Speak like a tasteful friend setting a table.

### Sample lines
- “A candle for rooms that need a little amber.”
- “Choose a scent. Choose its color. Make it feel like home.”
- “Soft rose, clean cedar, a little window light.”
- “For the hour when the lamps come on.”
- “Build a candle around a mood, a room, or a memory.”
- “Poured slowly. Lit often.”
- “Color-matched to the way it smells.”
- “Add a note. We’ll tuck it into the box.”

### PDP “smells like” examples
- **Saffron Dusk:** “warm spice, suede chair, the last orange light on the wall.”
- **Linen Fig:** “fresh sheets, green fig, a quiet Sunday window.”
- **Cedar Milk:** “pale wood, steamed milk, a book left open.”

## Anti-patterns

1. **No pure black/white luxury clone.** It will disappear beside Diptyque, Maison Louis Marie, APOTHEKE, and Brooklyn Candle Studio.[1][3][5]
2. **No flat digital gradients as the main brand idea.** Color must appear as wax, paper, glass, shadow, pigment, textile.
3. **No spa clichés:** stacked stones, eucalyptus shower, beige robe, anonymous bathroom.
4. **No fragrance pyramid overload.** Keep notes visible, but lead with mood and room use.
5. **No fake heritage.** Don’t invent Paris, apothecary archives, or 1800s perfumery codes.
6. **No loud Gen-Z maximalism.** Boy Smells already owns bolder cultural scent energy; Color & Scent should be calmer, warmer, more interior-led.[2]
7. **No sterile renders.** If the product is not ready, mock it physically with paper, wax, and glass rather than CGI.
8. **No generic “elevate your space” as hero language.** Competitors already use that language; if used, it must be buried in SEO copy, not brand copy.[3]

## Implementation north star

If one screen has to prove the new direction, build this: a warm editorial hero with a candle on a linen-covered table, colored swatch cards, visible flame, and a hand writing a scent name. Headline: **“Scent, in color.”** Subhead: **“Candles poured for the rooms, rituals, and moods you want to keep.”** CTA: **“Build yours”** and **“Shop by mood.”**

The brand should feel like a small object from a beautiful home — tactile, useful, personal, and photographable — not a digital candle store with nice fonts.
